*Researched 28 October 2026. Method: Perplexity sonar-pro (sequential calls), plus direct checks of primary pages where reachable. AICD CPD page and LinkedIn ad specs were checked directly. AHRC, DTA, Our Watch and Qld Government PDFs/pages blocked direct fetch (403/404/503 or empty), so those points rest on Perplexity's reading of the primary URLs cited. *Evidence labels used below: **STRONG** = peer-reviewed meta-analysis or large-scale study; **MODERATE** = single peer-reviewed studies / reviews with caveats; **PRACTICE** = regulator, peak-body or vendor guidance; **INFERENCE** = my reasoning, not sourced.*

## Q1. What do board members and executives realistically complete online? Module length, microlearning and completion rates

### Takeaway
No study gives an "ideal" length for directors specifically. The best evidence supports **short videos (≤6 min), segmenting content into learner-paced chunks, and one objective per chunk**. Voluntary self-paced e-learning usually has low completion unless it is short, relevant and tied to a purpose. The defensible proposal is **two modules of about 25–40 minutes each, built from 5–8 minute sections** that people can complete across sittings. Capyble's reported 70–80% completion on its DAF module would be well above voluntary benchmarks, so it is worth citing (with its source) as internal evidence.

### Cited Findings
- **STRONG (observational):** Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014) analysed 6.9 million video-watching sessions across four edX MOOCs. Median engagement topped out at about 6 minutes whatever the video's length. Viewers watched close to 100% of videos under 6 min, about 50% of 9–12 min videos and about 20% of 12–40 min videos. This measured engagement in MOOCs, not learning in professionals. — [Guo et al. 2014, L@S](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)
- **STRONG (meta-analysis):** Rey et al. (2019), *Educational Psychology Review* 31:389–419, pooled 56 investigations (88 comparisons). Segmenting multimedia material gave **small-to-medium gains in retention and transfer** and reduced cognitive load. Learners with high prior knowledge gained more on retention, which is relevant because board members and executives are experienced professionals. — [Rey et al. 2019 (MPI record)](https://is.tuebingen.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting); [DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-018-9456-4)
- **MODERATE (review):** A 2024 review in *Smart Learning Environments* found no agreed length for microlearning. Definitions run from 30 seconds–5 minutes, through lessons up to 15 minutes, to "under 10 minutes". It also cites evidence that attention drops in videos over about 9 minutes. — [Springer 2024, s40561-024-00334-5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-024-00334-5)
- **STRONG (large-scale):** Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente (2019, *Science*), covering 5.63M learners and 12.67M registrations on HarvardX/MITx from 2012–18, found **3.13% completion overall in 2017–18** and about **46% among paid verified-certificate learners**. This is a self-selection association, not a causal effect of certificates. — reported via [Inciarte summary](https://www.humbertoinciarte.com/research/completion-crisis/) **[verify against Science 363(6423):130–131]**
- **PRACTICE (industry, weak):** Industry summaries put voluntary self-paced workplace e-learning at about 30–40% completion, cohort-based courses at 60–80%, and mandatory compliance training at 70–95%+. Vendors claim around 80% for microlearning against 20–30% for long-form courses. These are not independent or standardised figures. — [Teachfloor](https://www.teachfloor.com/blog/employee-training-statistics); [Forasoft](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/elearning-video/articles-elearning/completion-rate-defining-measuring-improving); [SkillStudio](https://www.skillstudio.ai/compliance-training/corporate-elearning-statistics-2026-key-trends-roi-and-compliance-insights)
- **PRACTICE (survey, Australian):** AICD's NFP Governance and Performance Study 2025–26 (n=2,088) reports a median of **3 days a month** on a single NFP board, up from 2 days over seven years. 46% spend more than 3 days a month. — [AICD NFP Study 2025–26](https://www.aicd.com.au/content/dam/aicd/pdf/news-media/research/2026/nfp-performance-study-2025-26.pdf) **[verify; Perplexity-reported]**
- **PRACTICE (survey):** AICD's 2019 NFP study reported that about nine in ten NFP directors did training related to their role in the previous year. The most common amount was 3–7 days (26%), and 19% did more than a week. This shows directors do invest in learning, though the data are older (2019). — [AICD NFP Study 2019](https://www.aicd.com.au/content/dam/aicd/pdf/news-media/research/2019/07277-ADV-NFP-Governance-Performance-Study-2019-A4-68pp-WEB-2.pdf) **[verify]**
- **PRACTICE:** WorkUP Queensland's *Feminist Governance Toolkit* for boards of Qld specialist gendered-violence services is meant to be used "in chunks or sections", including in board induction and ongoing learning. It is designed for busy board members and says it complements, rather than replaces, governance training. — [WorkUP Qld](https://workup.org.au/resources/feminist-governance-toolkit/)

### Inferences
- **INFERENCE (proposal position):** Module 1 (boards) should take about **25–30 minutes**: 4–5 sections of 5–7 minutes, each with one 2–4 minute video and one decision point. Module 2 (executives and managers) should take about **35–45 minutes**: 5–6 sections, with more scenario practice. Both should be **resumable** and **completable across two sittings**. Each section should also work as a standalone item that can be **run as a 10-minute board-meeting agenda item**, which mirrors WorkUP's "chunks" model.
- **INFERENCE:** The board module should be framed as "one board meeting's worth of learning" or "one sitting". Do not frame it as a course. Directors already give about 3 days a month, so short and relevant beats comprehensive.
- **INFERENCE:** Completion will depend more on **how QTWAV distributes the modules** than on their length. Examples are a CEO or chair asking the board to complete before a meeting, use in induction, and linking to face-to-face sessions as pre-work. Cohort and deadline mechanisms are the strongest completion levers in the practice literature.
- **INFERENCE:** Capyble's 70–80% DAF completion figure should be described with its context: whether completion was voluntary or required, and how "completion" was defined. Otherwise it may look inflated against voluntary benchmarks.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found on online learning length or completion for **board directors or senior executives** in particular. All length evidence comes from general or MOOC populations.
- I could not find completion data for any Australian NFP governance e-learning (Our Community/ICDA, WorkUP, Governance Institute on-demand).
- Exact Rey et al. effect sizes were not retrieved. "Small-to-medium" comes from the abstract.

## Q2. How do governance/director education providers structure short board education? Is CPD recognition realistic or useful?

### Takeaway
Australian governance providers use either **multi-hour assessed short courses** (Governance Institute, about 3.5 h = 6 CPD; AICD full-day courses = 10 CPD points) or **self-directed toolkits and resources** (WorkUP, AICD guides and webinars). A 25–40 min module will not attract formal accreditation. It does not need to: **AICD members (MAICD/GAICD/FAICD) must earn 20 CPD points a year and may self-claim relevant external learning**, and Rainbow Tick requires training registers that include the governing body. A **completion certificate stating duration and learning outcomes** is cheap and gives real value.

### Cited Findings
- **PRACTICE (verified directly):** AICD Members, Graduates and Fellows "are required to accrue 20 CPD points each year". Affiliates, Life Fellows, reduced-rate and retired members are exempt. Points can be earned from "Formal activities from the AICD and other organisations of appropriate professional standing" and from "informal learning—such as briefings, reading, practical application and reflection". External activities are self-recorded in the member portal (type, provider, Framework quadrant, points claimed). Members should "retain any documentation (e.g. certificate of completion)" because AICD audits monthly. — [AICD CPD page](https://www.aicd.com.au/membership/continuing-professional-development.html)
- **PRACTICE:** The AICD Professional Development Handbook reportedly also describes **60 DPD units over a rolling three-year cycle**. — [AICD PD Handbook](https://www.aicd.com.au/content/dam/aicd/pdf/courses-programs/professional-development-handbook/professional-development-handbook.pdf) **[verify; may be superseded by the 20-points/year rule]**. The points-per-hour conversion is in a separate CPD points table that I did not retrieve.
- **PRACTICE:** AICD *Foundations of Directorship* is three one-day courses (Governance; Risk & Strategy; Finance). Each needs about 10 hours of pre-reading and is worth 10 CPD points, with 5 more for the optional assessment. — [AICD Foundations](https://www.aicd.com.au/courses-and-programs/all-courses/foundations-of-directorship.html) **[verify]**
- **PRACTICE:** Governance Institute of Australia short courses (e.g. *Not-for-Profit Governance Fundamentals*) run about 3.5 hours, are self-paced online or facilitated, and carry 6 CPD hours. The on-demand version ends with an MCQ quiz (75% pass, unlimited attempts). Six courses stack into a certificate. — [Governance Institute NFP Governance Fundamentals](https://www.governanceinstitute.com.au/short-courses/not-for-profit-governance-fundamentals/); [Certificate in Board Governance](https://www.governanceinstitute.com.au/certificates/certificate-in-board-governance/)
- **PRACTICE:** AICD's short-form board resources on this topic are a guide and a webinar rather than a module: *A director's guide to the positive duty to prevent workplace sexual harassment* (2023, updated 2025) with questions for directors to ask management, plus a webinar (2 Nov 2023). — [AICD director's guide](https://www.aicd.com.au/organisational-culture/business-ethics/change/a-directors-guide-to-the-positive-duty-to-prevent-workplace-sexual-harassment.html); [AICD webinar](https://www.aicd.com.au/courses-and-programs/all-webinars/WEB0002-H231101-01.html); [AICD Governing WHS psychosocial risks](https://www.aicd.com.au/content/dam/aicd/pdf/tools-resources/director-tools/organisation/governing-whs-psychosocial-risks-web.pdf)
- **PRACTICE:** AASW CPD can be met through activities from "AASW and/or external providers" if relevant to the member's practice. This matters because many DFSV managers are social workers. — [AASW CPD overview](https://www.aasw.asn.au/professional/cpd/cpd-overview/)
- **PRACTICE:** Under Rainbow Tick (2020 standards; Rainbow Health Australia, assessed by QIP), **Standard 2 Workforce development** expects organisations to identify LGBTIQ-inclusion training needs for the **governing body, managers, staff and volunteers**, keep a **training register**, and show that **at least 80% of personnel have done at least one basic LGBTIQ-inclusive practice session**. — [Rainbow Tick Guide](https://rainbowhealthaustralia.org.au/media/pages/rainbow-tick/2565067543-1709686053/rainbow-tick-guide-to-lgbti-inclusive-practice-web.pdf); [QIP Rainbow Tick](https://www.qip.com.au/standards/rainbow-tick-standards/) **[verify the 80% figure in the guide]**
- **PRACTICE:** Rise 360 can publish to SCORM/xAPI/cmi5 for LMS completion tracking and supports a completion/certificate workflow. Exact behaviour depends on publishing and LMS settings. — [Articulate accessibility & features](https://www.articulate.com/about/accessibility/) (Perplexity synthesis) **[verify certificate feature in current Rise]**

### Inferences
- **INFERENCE (proposal position on (c)):** Offer a **certificate of completion** (learner name, date, module title, "approx. 30/40 minutes", the 3 learning outcomes, issued by QTWAV/QC). Add a line such as *"AICD members may record this as self-directed/external learning toward annual CPD points"*. **Do not claim accreditation, endorsement or a set number of points**, and do not use AICD branding. The certificate also gives organisations evidence for **Rainbow Tick Standard 2 training registers**, **AHRC positive duty Standard 3 (Knowledge)** and WGEA GEI 6 training questions. That supports the value case for boards.
- **INFERENCE:** Formal CPD accreditation (e.g. Governance Institute or AASW-endorsed) is **not realistic or cost-effective** at $40k, and short modules fall below typical accredited thresholds of about 3.5 hours.
- **INFERENCE:** The board module should borrow AICD's proven device of **"questions directors should ask management"**, adapted to LGBTIQ+ inclusion. It could be a downloadable one-page board checklist, which also helps the module last after completion.
- **INFERENCE:** If QTWAV hosts on an LMS, use xAPI/SCORM completion. If it hosts as a Rise web link with no LMS, completion data will be limited. In that case use an end-of-module form or certificate request to count completions. Settle this hosting decision before quoting.

### Gaps
- I could not verify Our Community / ICDA's short online course formats, lengths or certificate rules.
- The number of modules and completion time for the WorkUP Feminist Governance Toolkit were not found.
- Governance Institute's member CPD rule (20 hours?) was not verified.
- I did not retrieve the AICD points-per-hour table.

## Q3. Example learning outcomes for board-level and manager-level inclusion/positive duty training, with drafts for each module

### Takeaway
The AHRC, Our Watch, Safe Work and WGEA frameworks all ask boards to **understand, resource, oversee and verify**, and ask managers to **identify risks, act, respond safely and prevent victimisation**. Outcomes should use observable verbs (identify, explain, ask, apply, plan) tied to governance or management acts, not "understand" or "be aware of". The AHRC warns that one-off training is unlikely to change behaviour by itself. That supports positioning the modules as **one part** of QTWAV's face-to-face program.

### Cited Findings
- **PRACTICE (regulator):** AHRC's Positive Duty Guidelines set **seven Standards**: Leadership, Culture, Knowledge, Risk management, Support, Reporting and response, and Monitoring, evaluation and transparency. Under Leadership, senior leaders understand their obligations under the Sex Discrimination Act, keep their knowledge of relevant unlawful conduct current, make sure measures are developed, documented, communicated and implemented, and review whether they work. — [AHRC fact sheet: steps to meet the positive duty](https://humanrights.gov.au/sites/default/files/2310_fs_steps_to_meet_the_positive_duty_v2_0.pdf)
- **PRACTICE (regulator):** AHRC's *Effective education and training* fact sheet (reported as updated 25 Feb 2025) accepts "formal face-to-face or virtual workshops, on-the-job training and e-learning courses" suited to people's roles. It warns that "A single educational campaign or a one-off training session is unlikely to result in long-term change". It says education works best when activities are used together, and each activity "should be evidence-based" and "regularly evaluated to ensure that it is effective". — [AHRC Effective education and training](https://humanrights.gov.au/sites/default/files/factsheet_-_effective_education_and_training_0_0_0.pdf) **[verify quotes; direct fetch returned 403]**
- **PRACTICE:** Our Watch *Workplace Equality and Respect* Standards have five standards: **Commitment, Conditions, Culture, Support, Resourcing**. Leadership criteria say leaders, "including board members and senior executives", take responsibility, sponsor policies and role-model respect. Capability criteria say managers and key contacts are trained to respond to disclosures of DFV and sexual harassment. The readiness tool asks whether "leadership (CEO/board members/senior executives)" has committed. — [Our Watch Workplace Standards (docx)](https://assets.ourwatch.org.au/assets/Workplace-resources/Workplace-Standards.docx); [WER Readiness assessment tool](https://assets.ourwatch.org.au/assets/Workplace-resources/WER-Readiness-assessment-tool.docx) **[verify; earlier Perplexity answer gave a different, uncited five-domain list, which I treat as unreliable]**
- **PRACTICE (WHS):** Safe Work Australia's *Model Code of Practice: Sexual and gender-based harassment* (2023) names senior leaders who are **officers**, including company directors, as having a **due diligence** duty to ensure the PCBU complies. Consultation with workers is needed at each stage of risk management. — [SWA model code](https://www.safeworkaustralia.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-12/model_code_of_practice_-_sexual_and_gender-based_harassment.pdf)
- **PRACTICE (WHS, Qld):** Queensland's *Managing the risk of psychosocial hazards at work* Code of Practice (2023) treats harassment (including sexual and gender-based harassment), bullying, conflict and poor organisational justice as psychosocial hazards, managed through identify, assess, control and review, with worker consultation. — (Perplexity synthesis; no URL returned) **[verify at worksafe.qld.gov.au]**
- **PRACTICE (statutory reporting):** Employers with 100+ employees report to WGEA. GEI 6 covers sexual harassment, harassment on the ground of sex and discrimination, including training frequency and content and reporting to the governing body. In 2024, 85% of employers had their SH policy reviewed by the CEO but only **55% by the board**. — [WGEA 2024 SH data](https://www.wgea.gov.au/newsroom/2024-new-data-workplace-sexual-harassment); [WGEA reforms factsheet](https://www.wgea.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/Gender-Equality-Reporting-Changes-Factsheet.pdf)
- **PRACTICE (governance):** AICD's director guide frames board oversight as a set of questions to management: who is accountable, how often the board gets reports, what data show, and whether low complaint numbers reflect low prevalence or low trust in reporting. — [AICD director's guide](https://www.aicd.com.au/organisational-culture/business-ethics/change/a-directors-guide-to-the-positive-duty-to-prevent-workplace-sexual-harassment.html)
- **Context data (survey, MODERATE):** In DCA/RMIT *Out at Work* (2018, n>1,600), 74% of LGBTIQ+ workers said being out at work was important but only 32% were out to everyone. Workers not out to everyone were twice as likely to feel down and 45% less likely to be satisfied with their job. **28% of trans/gender-diverse workers were out to no one**, against 4% of LGB workers. — [DCA Out at Work](https://www.dca.org.au/research/lgbtiq-out-at-work) (2018; older)
- **Context data (survey):** In *Private Lives 3* (La Trobe ARCSHS, 2020), 60.7% reported ever experiencing intimate partner violence and 43.2% reported violence from a family-of-origin member. — [Private Lives 3](https://www.latrobe.edu.au/arcshs/documents/arcshs-research-publications/Private-Lives-3.pdf); [AIHW LGBTIQA+ FDSV](https://www.aihw.gov.au/family-domestic-and-sexual-violence/population-groups/lgbtiqa-people)

### Inferences — draft measurable outcomes (INFERENCE; modelled on AHRC Standards, Our Watch criteria and AICD director questions)

**Module 1 — Boards: governing for queer and trans inclusion** (after completing, a board member will be able to):
1. **Identify** at least three places in their organisation's governing documents and arrangements (constitution or objects, eligibility and service criteria, funding contracts, policies) where LGBTIQ+ people may be excluded or made invisible, and **explain** why each is a governance risk as well as an HR issue. *(AHRC Standard 1 Leadership / 4 Risk; Our Watch Commitment and Conditions)*
2. **Pose** at least five specific questions to the CEO or management about inclusion of queer and trans staff and clients (e.g. accountability, data, reporting pathways, victimisation safeguards), and **recognise** what a strong and a weak answer looks like. *(AHRC Standard 7; AICD director-question model)*
3. **Describe** the board's role in **leading on values**: what visible board-level commitment looks like, and one action the board could take within 12 months (e.g. a policy review, inclusion in a strategic plan, or a reporting line to the board). *(Our Watch Commitment and Resourcing; WGEA GEI 6 board oversight)*
- *Wording rule:* say "know when to seek legal advice on anti-discrimination obligations", not "comply with the law". This keeps the module on the right side of the no-legal-advice constraint.

**Module 2 — Executives and managers: positive duty, psychosocial safety and the case for queer and trans workers** (after completing, a manager will be able to):
1. **Identify** psychosocial hazards particular to or heightened for queer and trans staff in DFSV work (e.g. misgendering, forced outing, minority stress, exposure to homophobic or transphobic clients, tokenistic "diversity load"), and **apply** identify, assess, control and review to one realistic scenario. *(Qld psychosocial Code 2023; SWA SGBH Code; AHRC Standard 4)*
2. **Choose and justify** an appropriate response when a staff member discloses harassment or discrimination, including steps that **prevent victimisation** and protect privacy (no outing), demonstrated through branching scenario decisions. *(AHRC Standards 5–6; Our Watch Support)*
3. **Articulate** an asset-based case for recruiting and retaining queer and trans workers (lived expertise, community trust, service reach to LGBTIQ+ victim-survivors), and **identify** two changes to their own recruitment, onboarding or supervision practice. *(Our Watch Conditions/Culture; DCA Out at Work)*
- *Measurement (INFERENCE):* outcome 2 is checked through scenario choices with feedback. Outcomes 1 and 3 are checked through a short applied question or a downloadable action-plan prompt. A **pre/post confidence self-rating** (3 items) gives cheap evaluation data and meets AHRC's "regularly evaluated" expectation.

### Gaps
- **Legal scope needs checking by another researcher:** the Commonwealth positive duty (SDA s47C) covers sex discrimination, sexual harassment, sex-based harassment, hostile workplace environments and victimisation. My understanding (unverified here) is that it **does not explicitly cover sexual orientation or gender identity discrimination**, though harassment of trans and queer staff can be sex-based harassment. Queensland's positive duty under the Anti-Discrimination Act / Respect at Work amendments has had commencement changes. **The Module 2 outcomes must be worded to match the verified legal position.**
- I could not retrieve any Respect@Work Council page with board-specific learning outcomes.
- I found no published Australian learning-outcome set specifically for LGBTIQ+ inclusion at board level (Pride in Diversity materials are member-only).

## Q4. Scenario-based learning; illustrated-with-voice vs filmed-with-actors; LGBTIQ+ and trans representation

### Takeaway
**Active decision-making beats passive viewing**, so scenarios are worth their cost. **Visual realism does not by itself improve learning**: low-fidelity simulation matches high-fidelity in most studies, and animation is at least as good as live action for knowledge. That makes **illustrated scenarios with voice acting (ideally trans and queer voice actors) the defensible default** for this budget and topic. They are cheaper (roughly a third to half the cost per finished minute), easier to update, safer for anonymity and emotional safety, and avoid the casting risks of portraying trans characters on screen. Real human faces should come from the **filmed peer interviews**. This hybrid uses the authenticity of real people where it works best (testimony and contact effects) and illustration where it works best (hypothetical, sensitive and branching scenes).

### Cited Findings
- **STRONG (meta-analysis):** Bezrukova et al. (2016, *Psychological Bulletin*), 260 samples over 40+ years: overall diversity-training effect g=0.38. Reactions g=0.61, cognitive learning g=0.57, behavioural g=0.48, attitudinal/affective g=0.30. Cognitive effects lasted and affective effects decayed. Effects were larger when training **combined awareness with skills**, ran longer, and sat **alongside wider organisational initiatives**. — [Bezrukova et al. 2016 (APA)](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/bul0000067)
- **PROVISIONAL:** A reported 2025 meta-analysis of 54 studies found active learning embedded in video beat passive viewing on retention (g=0.33), comprehension (g=0.28) and transfer (g=0.43). It was surfaced only via a practitioner blog. — [Visla blog](https://www.visla.us/blog/how-tos/how-to-use-interactive-videos-for-training/) **[unverified; do not cite in proposal without the original]**
- **MODERATE (experiment):** Stanford VHIL ran a sexual-harassment bystander experiment (N=100) comparing VR practice with 2D video. VR raised intentions to use indirect interventions but not knowledge, attitudes or reporting intentions. Practice improves some outcomes, not all. — [Stanford VHIL](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/empathydiversity/sexual-harassment-bystander-training-effectiveness-experimentally)
- **STRONG (meta-review):** Norman, Dore & Grierson (2012, *Medical Education*), 24 comparative studies: both high- and low-fidelity simulation improved performance over no intervention. Nearly all studies found **no significant advantage for high fidelity**, with average differences of about 1–2%. — [Norman et al. 2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2012.04243.x). A later review found low fidelity comparable in 15 of 17 studies. — [PMC7246118](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7246118/) (older evidence, from clinical skills)
- **MODERATE (systematic review):** In a review of animation in health-professions education (*Perspectives on Medical Education*, 2022), 8 of 10 knowledge studies favoured animation. The authors called the trial base small. — [Springer s40037-022-00736-6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40037-022-00736-6)
- **MODERATE (meta-analysis):** A meta-analysis of 21 studies of animated video against standard education found d=0.35. Note the comparator was standard education, not matched live action. — [ERIC EJ1371793](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1371793) **[verify citation: reported as *Patient Education and Counseling* 2023]**
- **MODERATE:** Mayer's **embodiment principle**: on-screen agents that use human-like gesture, gaze and expression improved transfer in 16 of 17 comparisons (median d=0.58). The **image principle** is that simply showing a speaker's face does not reliably improve learning. A meta-analysis found animated pedagogical agents gave transfer g≈0.45. Realism matters less than meaningful social cues. — [Mayer, research-based principles (UNH PDF)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf); [Cambridge Handbook ch. on pedagogical agents](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/multimedia-learning-with-animated-pedagogical-agents/B629E8948494D949DB818B4AB31F3B4F)
- **STRONG (field RCT):** Broockman & Kalla (2016, *Science*): about 10-minute perspective-taking conversations reduced anti-transgender prejudice among 501 voters, lasting at least 3 months. Trans and cis canvassers were both effective. Later field experiments included a **video-narrative condition** that also reduced exclusionary attitudes. — [Broockman & Kalla 2016](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~broockma/broockman_kalla_transphobia_canvassing_experiment.pdf); [Kalla & Broockman APSR](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/reducing-exclusionary-attitudes-through-interpersonal-conversation-evidence-from-three-field-experiments/4AA5B97806A4CAFBAB0651F5DAD8F223/share/e0daeef3010c1ee77a7b10d603eafac028bd40a7)
- **MODERATE:** Two studies (N=254, N=161) found transgender narratives reduced prejudice through narrative transportation, empathy and lower intergroup anxiety. — [Rodríguez-de-Dios & Soto-Sanfiel 2024, Cyberpsychology](https://cyberpsychology.eu/article/download/36908/33508/69080). A 2021 parasocial-contact experiment found the **quality** of the portrayal decisive, and poor parasocial contact can backfire. — [Communication Studies 2021](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10510974.2021.1876125)
- **PRACTICE (representation):** GLAAD's entertainment guidance says to **cast trans actors in trans roles**, because casting cis actors reinforces the "disguised" stereotype, and to involve trans writers and consultants. When a character misgenders someone, the misgendering should be corrected on screen. — [GLAAD Guide for LGBTQ Inclusion in Entertainment](https://assets.glaad.org/m/6b239e91c8c5d18c/original/Guide-for-LGBTQ-Inclusion-in-Entertainment-English.pdf); [GLAAD Media Reference Guide – transgender](https://glaad.org/reference/transgender/)
- **PRACTICE (Australian):** Our Watch guidance on reporting violence against trans and gender-diverse communities: do not reduce trans people to appearance or use "before and after" images; avoid sensational language; keep perpetrator responsibility visible; consult communities; show connection and contribution, not only victimhood. — [Our Watch guidelines (PDF)](https://assets.ourwatch.org.au/assets/Media-resources/OW_Guidelines_reporting_VA_trans_gender_communities_WEB.pdf); ACON/TransHub *Trans-Affirming Language Guide* (2020) — [TransHub](https://www.transhub.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Trans-Affirming-Language-Guide-ACON-2020.pdf)
- **PRACTICE (cost, Australian vendors; weak):** Template or Vyond-style animation costs about **A$1,200 ex GST per finished minute**, and custom animation with voice actors about **A$4,000/min**. A small live shoot starts around A$3,500 ex GST for half a day. Simple live-action training videos run A$2,000–8,000 each, and multi-actor or multi-location videos A$10,000+. — [instructionaldesign.com.au](https://instructionaldesign.com.au/how-much-does-elearning-development-cost/); [Visual Domain](https://www.visualdomain.com.au/blog/how-much-does-training-video-production-cost-in-australia). Perplexity's extrapolation of **A$5,000–15,000+ per finished minute for filmed branching** content is an estimate, not a published rate.
- **PRACTICE (accessibility):** Rise 360 scenario blocks need author-supplied **scene descriptions** and alt text for screen-reader users. — [Articulate Rise accessibility journey](https://www.articulatesupport.com/article/Rise-360-Our-Accessibility-Journey)

### Inferences
- **INFERENCE (position on (d)): recommend illustrated scenarios with voice acting as the default**, for these reasons:
  1. **Learning parity:** fidelity and animation evidence shows no loss.
  2. **Budget:** at $40k total for two modules plus videos, filmed branching (every branch needs a new shot) would use up the budget. Illustrated branches are cheap to add.
  3. **Safety and anonymity:** no real sector worker or community member is identifiable in a harassment or outing scenario. This matters in a small Queensland queer DFSV workforce where people know each other.
  4. **Representation:** it avoids the GLAAD casting problem (finding and paying trans actors in Brisbane on a small budget, and their exposure in a scenario about harassment) while still allowing **casting trans and queer voice actors**, which follows the spirit of the GLAAD and Our Watch guidance.
  5. **Maintenance:** scripts and text can be edited when law or policy changes without reshoots.
  6. **Rise compatibility:** Rise's native scenario block is built for illustrated characters.
- **INFERENCE:** Use **filmed footage for the co-designed peer interviews** (real faces, real voices, sector credibility). This is where the contact, narrative and testimonial evidence applies, and it ties the module to people the audience may know.
- **INFERENCE:** Offer **filmed actors as a priced option** only if QTWAV specifically values it, for example one flagship non-branching filmed vignette of 2–3 min used also as the promo. Be clear about the cost and the casting obligations (trans actors for trans roles, a sensitivity reader or consultant, and on-set care).
- **INFERENCE (design rules for scenarios):** Co-write scripts with QTWAV members. Show trans characters with ordinary working lives and expertise, not only as targets. Show misgendering being corrected. Keep descriptions of violence non-graphic, with content notes and support details (QLife, 1800RESPECT, DV Connect). Choices should be realistic dilemmas (not obvious right/wrong answers), give consequence-based feedback, and allow retry. In illustration, avoid stereotyped visual codes for queer and trans people. Use a diverse character set chosen with co-design input.
- **INFERENCE:** Rely on research and practice guidance to make the case. Don't claim that illustration is "proven safer" emotionally; no RCT shows that.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed head-to-head RCT was found comparing **illustrated vs live-action branching scenarios for workplace conduct or DFSV training**.
- No evidence was found that directly measures **emotional safety or distress** for animation against live action on violence topics.
- There is no Australian-specific, independent production cost benchmark. Figures come from vendor blogs.
- Mardi Gras and Pride in Diversity media guidance was not located in accessible form.

## Q5. Short topic videos, social clips and promos for driving uptake

### Takeaway
Platform guidance converges on **15–30 seconds for promotional clips**, made **for sound-off viewing (captions plus on-screen text)** with the key message in the first seconds. Topic videos inside modules should stay **≤4 minutes, ideally ≤3** (Guo). No peer-reviewed evidence shows that trailers raise course enrolment. Promos should be judged on **clicks through to the module and completions**, not views.

### Cited Findings
- **PRACTICE (platform, verified directly):** LinkedIn: "We recommend uploading videos that are 15-30 seconds long", which makes them eligible for all feed placements. "Consider adding subtitles" (SRT). LinkedIn lists **4:5 as the recommended vertical ratio**; 9:16 is supported. — [LinkedIn video ad specs](https://www.linkedin.com/help/lms/answer/a424737). A related LinkedIn article advises showing "the most impactful content in the first 10 seconds".
- **PRACTICE (platform marketing claim):** LinkedIn reports that **7–15 second videos can lift completion by up to 300%** compared with longer videos, and that **about 80% of users watch video ads with sound off**. — [LinkedIn video ad tips](https://business.linkedin.com/advertise/ads/sponsored-content/video-ads/tips); [LinkedIn marketing terms](https://business.linkedin.com/advertise/resources/marketing-terms/video-ads) **[platform self-reported; verify wording]**
- **PRACTICE (platform):** Meta's Reels guidance stresses vertical 9:16 video, on-screen text and attention-grabbing, relatable content. It does not prescribe one duration. — [Meta Business Help](https://business.facebook.com/business/help/1708053352711643/?id=376980407544978)
- **STRONG (engagement):** Guo et al. (2014) found median engagement capped at about 6 min, with near-complete viewing under 6 min. This supports keeping 2–4 min topic videos. — [Guo et al. 2014](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)
- **No evidence found:** Perplexity found no peer-reviewed causal estimate of how much a course trailer or promo increases enrolment. — (search null result)

### Inferences
- **INFERENCE (position on (e)):** a tiered video ecosystem in which every asset is cut from the same interview footage.
  - **Promo/trailer (one per module, plus one overall):** a 30–45 s master, with **15 s and 6–10 s cut-downs**. Use **4:5 for LinkedIn** (the primary channel for board and executive audiences) and **9:16 for Instagram/Facebook Reels**. The first 2–3 seconds should name the audience and the benefit ("Board member at a DFSV service? 30 minutes to…"). End with one call to action and a short URL.
  - **Social clips (9:16, about 20–45 s):** one striking peer-interview line per clip ("what I wish my board knew…"), with burned-in captions. Release them as a 4–6 week series around launch and key dates: IDAHOBIT 17 May, Wear It Purple (late August), Trans Day of Visibility 31 March, and DV Prevention Month in Qld (May). Peers sharing their own clips on LinkedIn will probably reach directors better than QTWAV's own channels.
  - **Topic videos (2–4 min, 16:9 inside Rise):** also usable on their own in face-to-face sessions and board packs, which links online and face-to-face delivery.
- **INFERENCE:** Distribution through **sector intermediaries** (QC, WorkUP Qld, DVConnect networks, peak bodies' e-newsletters, AICD Qld NFP networks) will probably matter more for board uptake than organic social reach. A one-page "board pack insert" with a QR code is a low-cost channel aimed directly at boards.
- **INFERENCE:** Measure uptake through UTM-tagged links, so QTWAV can report which channel drove completions. That data will also support the Snow Foundation and future funding applications.

### Gaps
- No Australian NFP or DFSV sector benchmarks for promo video performance were found.
- No peer-reviewed evidence links trailers to enrolment or completion.
- Instagram and Facebook duration guidance in current Meta documentation was not directly verified.

## Q6. Accessibility norms: captions, transcripts, WCAG 2.2 AA, Easy English

### Takeaway
Specify **WCAG 2.2 Level AA** as the target. The Australian Government's digital standards cite 2.2 AA, Queensland requires "the latest version" of WCAG at A and AA (currently 2.2), and the AHRC expects accessibility. Rise 360 does **not** guarantee conformance: Articulate says it "broadly supports" WCAG 2.1 AA, and audio description and some interactions need workarounds. Budget for **human-checked captions, transcripts, and audio description or a described-video alternative**. Easy English is **practice guidance, not a WCAG requirement**. Given these audiences, a plain-language summary is proportionate. A full Easy English version is an optional extra.

### Cited Findings
- **STANDARD (W3C):** WCAG 1.2.2 Captions (Prerecorded) is Level A. 1.2.3 Audio Description or Media Alternative is Level A. **1.2.5 Audio Description (Prerecorded) is Level AA**, so AA requires audio description wherever visual information is not conveyed by the soundtrack. — (W3C WCAG; Perplexity synthesis) [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)
- **PRACTICE (Qld Government):** "Queensland Government agencies are required to meet the latest version of the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) both Level A and AA." No version number is named in the wording. — [Qld forgov: Make digital services accessible](https://www.forgov.qld.gov.au/information-technology/queensland-government-enterprise-architecture-qgea/qgea-directions-and-guidance/qgea-policies-standards-and-guidelines/digital-service-standard/make-digital-services-accessible) **[verify; direct fetch returned empty]**
- **PRACTICE (Cth):** The DTA Digital Experience Policy started 1 January 2025. The Digital Portal Standard reportedly requires **WCAG 2.2 AA**. The Digital Access Standard is about entry points, not WCAG. — [DTA Digital Portal Standard](https://architecture.digital.gov.au/standard/digital-portal-standard); [DTA announcement](https://www.dta.gov.au/articles/digital-experience-policy-and-standards-now-live-digitalgovau) **[verify]**. These apply to government agencies. QTWAV/QC are not bound, but they are the reference point for "sector norm".
- **PRACTICE (vendor):** Articulate says Rise 360 "broadly support[s]" **WCAG 2.1 AA** criteria. Known limitations: video blocks don't support audio description tracks (upload a described version instead); some keyboard and focus-order issues; audio-only blocks have no caption tracks; scenario blocks need author-written scene descriptions; drag-and-drop and sorting interactions create barriers. — [Articulate accessibility](https://www.articulate.com/about/accessibility/); [Rise 360 accessibility maturity plan](https://www.articulate.com/about/accessibility/rise-360-accessibility-maturity-plan/); [Univ. of Wisconsin KB testing (Aug 2025)](https://kb.wisc.edu/accessibility/149837)
- **PRACTICE:** Easy English (Scope Australia approach: short sentences, familiar words, picture support, user testing) and Easy Read (used by Australian Government agencies) are inclusion practices, not WCAG criteria. — (Perplexity synthesis; no primary URL retrieved) **[verify at scopeaust.org.au]**

### Inferences
- **INFERENCE (proposal accessibility spec):** Target WCAG 2.2 AA, tested at course level (keyboard-only, NVDA/VoiceOver, 400% zoom/reflow, contrast). Provide human-edited closed captions on every video and **burned-in captions on all social clips**, with a downloadable transcript for each video. Add audio description or a text-described alternative for the illustrated scenarios: scene description fields plus narrated context, which is easier with illustration than with filmed action. Avoid drag-and-drop and sorting interactions and use Rise's scenario, knowledge-check and labelled-graphic blocks with tested settings. Include content notes and support-service links on every module page. Use plain language throughout (about Year 8–10 reading level for managers; board content can assume more governance vocabulary).
- **INFERENCE:** **Easy English is not essential** for board and executive audiences. Offer a **1–2 page Easy English or plain-language summary of each module** as an optional add-on. That keeps faith with sector norms without diluting core content.
- **INFERENCE:** Include an **accessibility statement** in the module noting Rise's known limitations and the alternatives available (e.g. transcript PDF or Word version). This is better than claiming full conformance.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm from primary text whether the Qld Government formally names WCAG 2.2.
- There is no Australian DFSV-sector-specific accessibility standard for e-learning.
- No guidance was found on Auslan expectations for sector e-learning. Auslan is an optional extra that QTWAV may raise.
